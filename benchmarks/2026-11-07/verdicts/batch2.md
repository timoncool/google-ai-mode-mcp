Because 14 C is radioactive, it decays over time–in other words, older artifacts have less 14 C than younger ones. 14 C decays by a process called beta decay. During this process, an atom of 14 C decays into an atom of 14 N, during which one of the neutrons in the carbon atom becomes a proton. This increases the number of protons in the atom by one, creating a nitrogen atom rather than a carbon atom. An electron and an elementary particle, called an antineutrino, are also generated during this process. 

 

### The Concept of a Half Life

 The time it takes for 14 C to radioactively decay is described by its half-life. 14 C has a half-life of 5,730 years. In other words, after 5,730 years, only half of the original amount of 14 C remains in a sample of organic material. After an additional 5,730 years–or 11,460 years total–only a quarter of the 14 C remains. The amount of 14 C remaining is used to determine the age of organic materials. After 10 half-lives, or 57,300 years, the amount of 14 C remaining (<0.1%) becomes very difficult to detect. Thus fossil fuels, which are much much older than 50,000 years, have no 14 C remaining.

Basic Principles Carbon-14 ( 14C) is a cosmogenic * radioactive isotope of carbon that is produced at a more-or-less constant rate in Earth’s upper atmosphere by the reaction of neutrons generated by high energy cosmic rays with nitrogen-14 ( 14N): †

Like all radioisotopes, 14C decays exponentially at a rate that is proportional to the number of radiocarbon atoms in the sample (fig. 3). If N0 is the number of 14C atoms at time t = 0 (time of death) and N is the number remaining after time t, this process can be defined by the equation ln(N0/N) = λt in which λ is a constant equal to 0.693/T½ and ln denotes a natural logarithm. According to the [NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory](https://gml.noaa.gov/education/isotopes/decay.html), this means that every 5,730 years, half of the carbon-14 atoms in a sample undergo radioactive beta decay and transform into stable nitrogen-14: [2]

-   **After 1 half-life (5,730 years):** 50% of the original carbon-14 remains.

Question: Which enzyme does aspirin irreversibly inhibit, and by what mechanism?

=== OUTPUT M92 ===
Aspirin irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase (COX), also called prostaglandin H2 synthase. Both COX-1 and COX-2 are affected, though aspirin is slightly more selective for COX-1.

**Mechanism:** Aspirin is an acetylating agent. It transfers its acetyl group onto a serine in the enzyme's active site, which blocks the native substrate from binding. Sources give the serine as Ser530 in COX-1 and Ser516 in COX-2, though some report Ser529 for COX-1 because of different numbering. One study describes a substrate-assisted mechanism in which aspirin's own carboxyl group acts as a general base during the reaction. Another reports that the peroxidase activity of the enzyme regulates how readily acetylation occurs.

**Consequence:** Because the modification is covalent, the enzyme stays inactive. Platelets have no nucleus and cannot make new COX, so their function stays suppressed for their lifespan. This is why low-dose aspirin has a lasting antiplatelet effect, in contrast to reversible NSAIDs like ibuprofen.

Aspirin irreversibly inhibits **cyclooxygenase (COX)** enzymes—specifically both the **COX-1** and **COX-2** isoforms—via **covalent acetylation**. [1]

-   **Covalent Acetylation:** Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) transfers its acetyl group to a specific **serine residue** inside the enzyme's active site (such as Ser529 in human COX-1).
    
-   **Steric Hindrance:** This permanent modification physically blocks the cyclooxygenase channel. It prevents the natural substrate, **arachidonic acid**, from reaching the catalytic site. [2]
    
-   **Downstream Blockade:** Blocking arachidonic acid conversion halts the production of prostaglandin precursors (PGG₂ and PGH₂). This downstream blockade stops the synthesis of **prostaglandins** (mediators of pain, fever, and inflammation) and **thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂)** (a potent promoter of platelet aggregation). [3]
    

### Key Distinctions

-   **Irreversible vs. Reversible:** Unlike non-aspirin NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen or naproxen), which bind reversibly and compete with arachidonic acid, aspirin permanently disables the enzyme. The affected cell must synthesize entirely new COX enzymes to restore activity. [4]
    
-   **Lasting Antiplatelet Effect:** Because blood platelets lack a nucleus and cannot produce new COX-1 enzymes, a single dose of aspirin impairs platelet aggregation for the platelet's entire **7- to 10-day lifespan**. [5]
